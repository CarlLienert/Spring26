6.

For \(n=4\): \(4!=24\) and \(4^2=16\) so the inequality holds. Assume \(n!>n^2\) and consider:

\[\begin{align*} (n+1)!&=(n+1)\cdot n!\\ &>(n+1)\cdot n^2 \ \ \ \ \text{\it by the inductive hypothesis}\\ &>(n+1)\cdot (n+1)\\ &=(n+1)^2. \end{align*}\]

The second inequality requires that we prove \(n^2>n+1\) for \(n\geq 4\). The following inequalities are equivalent: \[\begin{align*} n^2&>n+1\\ n^2-n-1&>0\\ (n-2)(n+1)&>0.\\ \end{align*}\]

The last inequality is true if \(n<-1\) or \(n>2\). So, \(n^2>n+1\) for \(n\geq 4.\)

We have shown that \(n!>n^2\) implies \((n+1)!>(n+1)^2\), and we have shown the inequality is true for \(n=4\). By induction, \(n!>n^2\) is true for all integers \(n\geq 4\).

8.

For \(n=1\): \[2=\frac{2!}{1!}.\]

Assume the identity is true for \(n\) and consider:

\[\begin{align*} 2\cdot 6 \cdot 10 \cdots (4n-2)\cdot (4n+2)&=\frac{(2n)!}{n!}\cdot (4n+2)\\&=\frac{(2n)!\cdot 2 \cdot (2n+1)}{n!}\\ &= \frac{(2n)!\cdot 2 \cdot (2n+1)\cdot (n+1)}{n!\cdot (n+1)}\\ &= \frac{(2n)! \cdot (2n+1)\cdot (2n+2)}{n!\cdot (n+1)}\\ &=\frac{(2(n+1))!}{(n+1)!}=\frac{(2n+2)!}{(n+1)!} \end{align*}\] which shows the identity is true for \(n+1\). By induction the identity is true for all integers \(n\geq1\).

The inequality \[2^n(n!)^2\leq(2n)!\] is equivalent to \[2^n(n!) \leq \frac{(2n)!}{n!}\] so from part (a), we need to show that \[2^n(n!) \leq 2\cdot 6 \cdot 10 \cdots (4n-2).\]

The right hand side is: \[\begin{align*} 2\cdot 6 \cdot 10 \cdots (4n-2)&=(2\cdot1)\cdot(2\cdot3)\cdot(2\cdot 5)\cdots (2\cdot (2n-1))\\ &=2^n\cdot (1\cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdots (2n-1)) \end{align*}\]

Clearly \[n! \leq (1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot (2n-1))\] since there are \(n\) factors in both products and all but the first factor are greater in the right hand side. For example, if \(n=4\): \[1\cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \leq 1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7.\] Multiply both sides of \[n! \leq (1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot (2n-1))\] by \(2^n\) to obtain the desired result.

9.

For \(n=1\), \(1+a \geq 1+a\).

Assume \((1+a)^n \geq 1+na\) and consider \((1+a)^{n+1}\):

\[\begin{align*} (1+a)^{n+1}&=(1+a)^n(1+a)\\ &\geq (1+na)(1+a)\\ &=1+na+a+na^2\\ &=1+(n+1)a+na^2\\ &\geq 1+(n+1)a \end{align*}\]

The first inequality follows from the inductive hypothesis. This first inequality is also where we need to use the assumption that \(1+a >0\). If \(1+a<0\), the direction of the inequality would change. The second inequality is true since \(na^2\geq 0\).
